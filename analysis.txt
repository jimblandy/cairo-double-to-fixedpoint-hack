  Your hunch was right, for the common case. On independent conversions (the
  realistic case — converting arrays of coordinates), naive is ~2x faster, on
  every architecture level I tried, including plain SSE2. I checked the
  disassembly to see why: naive auto-vectorizes into packed vmulpd/vcvttpd2dq (8

  the compiler from vectorizing it at all.

  The one place the old trick still wins is the latency-bound case: a single
  addsd + movd (extracting the low 32 bits of the mantissa) has lower latency
  than a single mulsd + cvttsd2si. So if you're doing one conversion buried in a
  serial dependency chain with nothing else to overlap it with, magic-number is
  marginally faster (~15-20%) — but that's a narrow, somewhat artificial
  scenario compared to how Cairo actually uses this (converting many coordinates
  while rasterizing).

  So: the comment's claim was almost certainly true on 2003-era hardware without
  SSE2's cvttsd2si/SIMD float-to-int conversions and without compilers that
  auto-vectorize FP loops. On a modern compiler/CPU, the auto-vectorizer makes
  the "obvious" approach faster for realistic (batch) workloads, and only
  marginally slower in the worst case (isolated, serially-dependent
  conversions).

● Good instinct to double check — let me verify precisely what's different
  between the two loop bodies. I disassembled chain_naive and chain_magic and
  they're identical except for one instruction pair in the loop-carried
  dependency chain:

  naive:  vmulsd %xmm1,%xmm0,%xmm0   →   vcvttsd2si %xmm0,%eax
  magic:  vaddsd %xmm1,%xmm0,%xmm3   →   vmovq      %xmm3,%rax

  Everything else in the loop (the xor, inc, cwtl, the vcvtsi2sd converting back
  to double to continue the chain, loop control) is byte-for-byte identical
  between the two functions. So the latency delta we measured (~2.6ns vs ~3.2ns
  per iteration) is attributable entirely to that one pair of instructions.

  To your two sub-questions:

  "Aren't FP multiplies single-cycle?" No — that's conflating throughput with
  latency. On essentially every modern x86 core, addsd/mulsd are fully pipelined
  with high throughput (the FP unit can start a new one every cycle or every
  half-cycle), but each individual instruction still takes a few cycles to
  actually produce its result — commonly 3-4 cycles latency. You only get the
  "1-per-cycle" behavior when consecutive multiplies are independent, so the CPU
  can have several in flight at once. In a serial dependency chain — which is
  exactly what my latency benchmark forces — that pipelining is invisible; each
  iteration has to wait out the full latency of the previous one. That's the
  whole reason the throughput and latency benchmarks told different stories:
  naive wins on throughput (independent ops, pipeline/vectorize freely) but
  loses on latency (forced serial chain).

  "Shouldn't cvttsd2si also be cheap?" It's cheap in throughput terms (that's
  why it vectorizes so well as vcvttpd2dq), but per-instruction it's doing real
  work: decode the IEEE-754 sign/exponent/mantissa, shift according to the
  exponent, truncate toward zero, and detect out-of-range/NaN inputs to produce
  the "integer indefinite" result (0x80000000) per the x86 spec. That's a
  genuine format conversion through dedicated FP→int conversion logic. vmovq 
  %xmm,%rax, by contrast, does none of that — it's a raw copy of 64 bits from
  the vector register file to the general-purpose register file, with zero
  interpretation of what those bits mean numerically. Across x86
  microarchitectures, "real" numeric conversions between FP and integer domains
  have consistently carried a few cycles more latency than plain bit-transfer
  instructions between the same register files, precisely because of that extra
  decode/round/except-check logic.

  I wanted to give you exact cycle counts via perf stat, but this sandbox has
  perf_event_paranoid=4 (no perf access without root), so I can't pull hardware

  "conversion costs a couple more cycles than a bit-copy" pattern, but I can't
  give you a citation-grade cycle-exact number for this specific Zen4 core
  without that access.

● Your memory is correct — but for a different chip than the one I've been 
  benchmarking on.

  The effect you're recalling was very real and well-documented on Intel's early
  AVX-512 implementations, starting with Skylake-X/Skylake-SP (~2017). Those
  chips had multiple "AVX frequency license" levels — L0 (scalar/128-bit), L1
  (light 256/512-bit), L2 (heavy 512-bit, e.g. FMA) — with progressively lower
  max turbo clocks, plus a real transition penalty (tens of microseconds, worse
  on early steppings) when switching license levels. Sustained heavy 512-bit
  code really could run at a noticeably lower steady-state clock than scalar
  code on the same core, and mixed scalar/AVX-512 code paid repeated transition
  costs. It got better with Ice Lake and later Intel generations, and Intel
  actually fused AVX-512 off entirely on most client chips after Alder Lake
  because of the heterogeneous P/E-core design — so on client Intel it mostly
  went from "throttles" to "doesn't exist" for a few generations.

  This machine, though, is an AMD Threadripper PRO 7975WX — Zen4. AMD's Zen4
  implements AVX-512 by internally splitting every 512-bit instruction into two
  sequential 256-bit micro-ops on the existing 256-bit datapath
  ("double-pumping"), specifically so it doesn't need the extra power
  delivery/voltage domain that caused Intel's downclocking. There's no separate
  frequency license state at all.

  I checked empirically rather than just citing that: I built three versions of
  the throughput benchmark (AVX-512, AVX2-only via -mno-avx512f, and SSE2-only
  baseline via -march=x86-64-v2), ran each under perf stat with a long enough
  loop (5000 passes over the 1M-element array) that fixed startup cost is
  negligible, and derived clock speed from cycles / task-clock:

  ┌─────────┬──────────────────┬─────────┬───────────────────────┬─────────┐
  │  build  │      naive       │ naive   │ magic cycles/element  │ magic   │
  │         │  cycles/element  │  clock  │       (control)       │  clock  │
  ├─────────┼──────────────────┼─────────┼───────────────────────┼─────────┤
  │ AVX-512 │ 0.487            │ 5.00    │ 1.015                 │ 4.97    │
  │         │                  │ GHz     │                       │ GHz     │
  ├─────────┼──────────────────┼─────────┼───────────────────────┼─────────┤
  │ AVX2    │ 0.517            │ 4.95    │ 1.016                 │ 4.94    │
  │         │                  │ GHz     │                       │ GHz     │
  ├─────────┼──────────────────┼─────────┼───────────────────────┼─────────┤
  │ SSE2    │ 0.516            │ 4.88    │ 1.020                 │ 4.95    │
  │         │                  │ GHz     │                       │ GHz     │
  └─────────┴──────────────────┴─────────┴───────────────────────┴─────────┘

  magic never vectorizes regardless of target flag, so it's a built-in control —
  and its clock speed is flat (~4.9-5.0GHz) across all three builds. naive's
  clock speed is also flat across all three, including the AVX-512 build. No
  downclock, transient or sustained, is visible here. That matches AMD's stated
  design goal for Zen4.

  There's a second, more interesting finding buried in that table: AVX-512 only
  beats AVX2/SSE2 by about 6% (0.487 vs ~0.516 cycles/element), not the ~2x
  you'd expect from doubling register width — again consistent with
  double-pumping, since a "512-bit" op is really just two 256-bit ops back to
  back internally, so it saves on loop/instruction overhead but not on raw
  execution throughput.

  The practical upshot for your original question: the vectorization advantage
  that makes naive win doesn't depend on AVX-512 at all. The magic/naive
  throughput ratio is essentially the same (~2x) at SSE2, AVX2, and AVX-512 —
  SSE2 alone (present on every x86-64 CPU since 2003) is enough to vectorize the
  naive multiply-and-truncate and give it the win, since it's the ability to
  vectorize at all, not the specific width, that the union-based trick
  forecloses. So even on hardware with no AVX-512, or with an early Skylake-X
  chip paying the downclock tax you remember, naive's advantage would very
  likely survive — it just wouldn't get the (small, on this chip) extra boost
  from wider registers.
